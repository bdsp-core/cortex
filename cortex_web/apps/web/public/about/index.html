Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony, and Cass Sunstein describe noise as unwanted variability in judgments that should agree. In EEG interpretation, two physicians can inspect the same record and reach different conclusions because their thresholds for declaring a spike, seizure, or rhythmic pattern are not identical. That insight inspired CORTEX to estimate a learner profile with skill, bias, and residual noise rather than reduce performance to one score. Skill describes how reliably a reader separates clinically meaningful EEG patterns from plausible alternatives across domains. Bias describes the reader's response threshold: whether uncertain evidence is more likely to be called positive or negative. Together they distinguish not knowing from using a systematically liberal or conservative threshold. Continuous measurement lets those quantities update as evidence arrives, while Optimization directs the test toward the uncertainty that matters most. The resulting Response history becomes an interpretable record of competence, threshold, and residual noise.

At the same time, extraordinary resources are training models to detect EEG abnormalities. Physicians still need the expertise to verify outputs, recognize edge cases, and correct systems when distribution shift, artifact, rare morphology, or missing clinical context defeats automation. CORTEX treats assessment as a sequential Bayesian inference problem. A prior over domain-level skill and bias is represented by an SMC particle cloud. After each answer, the Test engine evaluates the response likelihood, reweights particles, monitors effective sample size, resamples when degeneracy increases, and can rejuvenate the posterior with Metropolis-Hastings steps related to Markov chain Monte Carlo. Expected information gain then selects the next EEG item, and posterior precision supports adaptive stopping when remaining uncertainty is small enough. That loop makes eXpertise measurable without administering every possible question. The approach is being implemented with epilepsy monitoring unit teams at Stanford, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, and Massachusetts General Hospital. We invite other EMUs, educators, and clinical neurophysiology programs to participate in validation, local calibration, and collaborative deployment.
